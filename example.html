Building ShopFlow
An E-Commerce Platform

A complete, stage-by-stage walkthrough of professional software engineering — from the first client meeting to production and evolution — applying every concept from Sommerville's Chapters 1–4. You are the Project Manager & Team Leader.

You — Project Manager / Scrum Master Kareem (Dev 1) — Backend Lead Sara (Dev 2) — Frontend Lead Omar (Dev 3) — Full-Stack Layla (Dev 4) — QA / Full-Stack Nadia — Client (Product Owner proxy)
Phase 0 — Week 1

Client Discovery & Feasibility

Before writing a single requirement or line of code, you need to understand whether this project is worth doing and what the client actually needs. This phase produces the Feasibility Study and sets up the first proper meeting.

Step 1 — Pre-Meeting Preparation

You receive an email from Nadia Al-Hassan, owner of a specialty food boutique. Before meeting her, you prepare an open interview framework — questions designed to understand her business, her pain points, and her vision without leading her toward any particular solution.

M
Pre-Discovery Interview Framework — Open Questions
DOC-001

Purpose: Understand the client's business context before any technical discussion. These are open questions — no yes/no answers, no leading toward a solution.

Opening questions:
"Tell me about your business — how does it operate today, from the moment a customer finds you to the moment they receive their product?"
"Walk me through a typical day in the shop — what tasks take up most of your time?"
"Where do your customers come from — walk-in, phone, social media, word of mouth?"

Pain-point questions:
"What parts of running the business frustrate you most right now?"
"Have you lost sales you think a website could have captured? Tell me about one."
"How do you manage orders, payments, and stock today — what tools do you use?"

Vision questions:
"If you imagine your business in 2 years with a new system — what does a perfect day look like?"
"What does success look like for this project?"
"Are there any other websites or shops you admire — what do they do well?"

Constraint questions (closed, for budget/schedule):
"Do you have a budget range in mind?"
"Is there a date you need this live by — a launch event, a season?"
"Do you have a team member who can manage the system day-to-day after launch?"

Step 2 — The First Client Meeting (Ethnographic Discovery)

You visit Nadia's shop in person for 2 hours. The first hour is an open interview — you let her talk. The second hour is ethnographic observation — you watch her take a phone order, package a product, and process a payment. This reveals things she would never have mentioned in an interview because they're second nature to her.

T
Discovery Meeting Transcript — Nadia's Specialty Foods
DOC-002

Location: Nadia's shop, Tuesday 9 April 2024 · Attendees: You (PM), Nadia (Client)

You Tell me about your business — how does it operate today, from the moment a customer finds you to the moment they receive their product?
Nadia Most customers find us on Instagram, honestly. They DM me, I reply, they tell me what they want — we have maybe 60 products. Then I check if I have it in stock, I tell them a price, they pay on Vodafone Cash or bank transfer, and I package it myself. If they're local, I deliver or they come pick up. For Cairo orders I use a courier.
You How long does that whole process take per order, roughly?
Nadia An order can take me 45 minutes just in WhatsApp back-and-forth. Sometimes I'm sleeping and a message comes at midnight. I wake up to 20 messages sometimes. And I've lost orders because I replied too late and they bought somewhere else.
You Walk me through a day — what takes the most time?
Nadia Honestly? Messages. Answering the same questions — "do you have X", "what's the price of Y", "can I order two of Z". And then at the end of the month I have to manually figure out what I sold, what I need to reorder — I use a notebook. I've run out of stock and didn't know until a customer asked.
You If you could wave a magic wand, what does a perfect Tuesday look like?
Nadia I open my laptop at 9am and I see: these orders came in last night, they're already paid, here's what needs to be packaged. I package them. That's it. No messages, no chasing payments, no guessing stock. And maybe customers can see my products any time, not just when I post on Instagram.
Ethnographic Observation Note (Hour 2)

While observing, you notice: (1) Nadia manually checks a physical notebook when asked about stock — a 4-minute process per inquiry. (2) When a phone order arrives, she has no way to check if a discount applies — she guesses. (3) She writes orders on sticky notes and transfers them to a ledger at day end — high risk of data loss. (4) Three customers ask about delivery cost — she calculates it manually each time. These observations reveal requirements she never mentioned: real-time stock visibility, automated delivery cost calculation, and order tracking. This is exactly what ethnography discovers that interviews miss.

Step 3 — Feasibility Study

Before writing requirements, you produce a short feasibility study. Per Sommerville, this answers three questions: (1) Does the system contribute to organisational objectives? (2) Can it be implemented within schedule and budget using current technology? (3) Can it integrate with existing systems?

FS
Feasibility Study — ShopFlow E-Commerce Platform
DOC-003

Project: ShopFlow — Custom E-Commerce Platform for Nadia's Specialty Foods
Date: 12 April 2024  |  Prepared by: [Your Name] — Project Manager
Client: Nadia Al-Hassan — Nadia's Specialty Foods

1. Organisational Feasibility
The client's primary pain points are: 45-minute order processing time via DM, lost sales due to response latency, manual stock management via notebook, and inability to capture night-time orders. An e-commerce system directly addresses all four. Expected outcome: reduce order processing to under 5 minutes, capture 100% of orders placed outside business hours, eliminate stock-tracking notebook. The system clearly contributes to the business objective of scaling revenue without hiring additional staff. FEASIBLE.

2. Technical Feasibility
This is a standard interactive transaction-based web application (Sommerville Type 2). Required technology is mature and well-understood: React.js (frontend), Node.js/Express (backend), PostgreSQL (database), Stripe (payment processing). No novel research required. Team has relevant experience. Estimated development time: 10 weeks across 5 sprints (2 weeks each). FEASIBLE.

3. Integration Feasibility
Required integrations: Stripe payment gateway (well-documented REST API), email service (SendGrid), shipping cost calculation (Aramex API — Egypt). No legacy systems need to be replaced; the shop's Instagram and phone channels can coexist during transition. The client uses a simple WhatsApp-based workflow today — no complex data migration required. FEASIBLE.

4. Financial Feasibility
Client budget: 80,000–100,000 EGP. Team estimate at scoping level: 10 weeks × 5 developers at blended rate ≈ 90,000 EGP (within range). Ongoing hosting: ~500 EGP/month. Stripe fees: 2.9% per transaction (passed to platform). ROI expected within 3–4 months based on client's stated 15–20 lost orders/month at average order value 450 EGP. FEASIBLE.

Recommendation: Proceed to full requirements engineering. The project is technically straightforward, organisationally valuable, financially viable, and integrations are low-risk. Risk areas: client availability for feedback (she is a solo operator), and defining the delivery integration scope clearly in Sprint 3.

Step 8 — Non-Functional Requirements (NFR)

Sommerville: "Non-functional requirements are often more critical than functional requirements — failing to meet an NFR can make the whole system unusable." All NFRs are written quantitatively so they can be objectively tested.

Product Requirements

PRODUCT NFR-P01
The product catalogue page shall load and display all products within 2 seconds on a standard 4G mobile connection (20 Mbps downlink).
Metric: Page load time measured via Lighthouse Performance audit. Pass threshold: ≥ 90/100 Performance score.
PRODUCT NFR-P02
The system shall handle a minimum of 100 concurrent users without degradation in response time exceeding 20% above baseline.
Metric: Load test using k6 with 100 VUs, baseline response time under 5 VUs. Degradation measured as p95 response time.
PRODUCT NFR-P03
The system shall achieve ≥ 99.5% uptime during business hours (08:00–22:00 EET, Sunday–Thursday). Planned downtime shall not exceed 30 minutes per month.
Metric: Uptime Robot monitoring. Calculated monthly: (total minutes - downtime minutes) / total minutes × 100.
PRODUCT NFR-P04
All customer-facing pages shall achieve a Lighthouse Accessibility score of ≥ 85. All form fields shall have associated labels and ARIA attributes.
Metric: Lighthouse Accessibility audit per page. Manual screen-reader test on checkout flow.
PRODUCT NFR-P05
A new admin user shall be able to add a product, update stock, and process an order without training, within 10 minutes of first login.
Metric: Usability test with one non-technical participant. Time recorded from first login to order status update.

Organisational Requirements

ORGANISATIONAL NFR-O01
All backend code shall use TypeScript. Test coverage for business-logic modules (cart, orders, stock) shall be ≥ 80% as measured by Jest's coverage reporter.
Metric: jest --coverage report checked in CI/CD pipeline. Build fails if coverage drops below 80%.
ORGANISATIONAL NFR-O02
The system shall be deployed on Vercel (frontend) and Railway (backend). All configuration shall be managed via environment variables — no credentials in source code.
Metric: Secrets scan (GitGuardian) in CI pipeline. Zero secrets in git history.

External / Regulatory Requirements

EXTERNAL NFR-E01
The system shall never store raw card numbers. All payment card data shall be handled exclusively by Stripe. The system shall be PCI-DSS compliant via Stripe's Level 1 certification.
Metric: No card data in server logs, database, or network requests beyond Stripe's tokenised payment intent ID.
EXTERNAL NFR-E02
All invoices generated by the system shall include 14% VAT as required by Egyptian tax law, with a separate VAT line item and the shop's tax registration number.
Metric: Invoice template review by client's accountant before go-live.
EXTERNAL NFR-E03
Customer personal data (name, address, phone) shall be stored in an encrypted database column. Data shall not be shared with third parties other than the payment gateway and shipping provider as necessary to fulfil orders.
Metric: Database column encryption verified. Privacy policy reviewed by client before launch.

Phase 2 — Week 3

System Architecture & Design

Before any code is written, you need a stable architecture. For an e-commerce system, the architecture must support incremental delivery, future evolution, and the non-functional requirements. This is one area where upfront planning pays off even in agile.

Key Architecture Decisions:
1. REST API (not GraphQL) — simpler for the team, sufficient for v1, easier to onboard future developers.
2. Separate customer-facing API from admin API routes — different authentication middleware, easier to audit.
3. PostgreSQL over MongoDB — order/product/stock data is highly relational; ACID transactions are required for stock management (FR-011, FR-012).
4. Frontend as SPA — enables fast page transitions, better mobile UX; SEO handled via Next.js server components for product pages.

Step 14 — Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

ADRs document important architectural decisions with their context, options considered, and rationale. They are critical for future maintenance — they explain why the system was built this way.

ADR-001 Use PostgreSQL for stock management ACCEPTED
Context

Stock management requires atomic decrements when orders are placed (FR-011). Two concurrent customers could purchase the last unit of a product simultaneously, causing overselling. This is a classic race condition problem.

Options Considered

A) MongoDB with application-level locking · B) Redis with atomic DECR commands · C) PostgreSQL with SELECT FOR UPDATE + transaction

Decision

C — PostgreSQL with row-level locking. A single SQL transaction with SELECT FOR UPDATE ensures atomicity without separate cache infrastructure.

Consequences

Team must be careful with long-running transactions (don't hold locks during Stripe API call). Stripe call happens outside the transaction; stock is decremented only after payment confirmation webhook.

ADR-002 Process payment via Stripe webhook, not redirect ACCEPTED
Context

Card payment confirmation must be reliable. Network failures between the payment provider redirect and our confirmation page can cause orders to be placed without confirmed payment (or vice versa).

Decision

Use Stripe's payment.succeeded webhook to confirm orders. The frontend shows a "pending" state; the webhook triggers order creation, stock decrement, and confirmation email. The frontend polls for order status.

Consequences

More complex flow. Webhook must be idempotent (use Stripe event ID as idempotency key). But prevents duplicate orders and guarantees order only created when payment is confirmed.

Test-First Development (TDD) Example

Per Sommerville and XP: tests are written before the code that implements the feature. Layla and Kareem practice this on BP-003 (Product API). The tests define the interface and expected behaviour before implementation begins.

TDD
TDD — Product Service Tests (written before implementation)
DOC-013
// products.service.test.ts  — written BEFORE products.service.ts
// Kareem writes these tests. They define the contract the implementation must fulfil.

import { getProducts, getProductById } from '../services/products.service'
import { prismaMock } from '../test/prisma.mock'

describe('ProductService', () => {

  describe('getProducts', () => {

    it('returns all active products with stock_qty included', async () => {
      const mockProducts = [
        { id: 'uuid-1', name: 'Almonds 500g', price: 85.00, stock_qty: 20, is_active: true },
        { id: 'uuid-2', name: 'Cashews 250g', price: 120.00, stock_qty: 0, is_active: true },
      ]
      prismaMock.product.findMany.mockResolvedValue(mockProducts)

      const result = await getProducts()

      expect(result).toHaveLength(2)
      expect(result[0]).toHaveProperty('stock_qty')  // FR-005 — must include stock
      expect(prismaMock.product.findMany).toHaveBeenCalledWith(
        expect.objectContaining({ where: { is_active: true } })
      )
    })

    it('filters by category_id when provided', async () => {
      prismaMock.product.findMany.mockResolvedValue([])
      await getProducts({ categoryId: 'cat-uuid-123' })
      expect(prismaMock.product.findMany).toHaveBeenCalledWith(
        expect.objectContaining({
          where: { is_active: true, category_id: 'cat-uuid-123' }
        })
      )
    })

    it('does not return inactive products', async () => {
      prismaMock.product.findMany.mockResolvedValue([])
      await getProducts()
      // Verify only is_active: true products are queried
      const call = prismaMock.product.findMany.mock.calls[0][0]
      expect(call.where.is_active).toBe(true)
    })
  })

  describe('getProductById', () => {

    it('returns a product when found', async () => {
      const mockProd = { id: 'uuid-1', name: 'Almonds', stock_qty: 20 }
      prismaMock.product.findUnique.mockResolvedValue(mockProd)
      const result = await getProductById('uuid-1')
      expect(result).toEqual(mockProd)
    })

    it('throws ProductNotFoundError when product does not exist', async () => {
      prismaMock.product.findUnique.mockResolvedValue(null)
      await expect(getProductById('non-existent'))
        .rejects.toThrow('ProductNotFoundError')
    })
  })
})

Step 21 — Formal Change Request (CR-001)

Three weeks after launch, Nadia requests a significant new feature. This goes through the formal three-stage change management process (Sommerville Ch. 4).

CR-001 WhatsApp Order Notifications for Admin

Stage 1 — Problem Analysis & Change Specification
Submitted by: Nadia Al-Hassan · Date: 26 July 2024
Request: "When a new order comes in, I want to receive a WhatsApp message with the order details, not just an email. I don't always check email immediately but I always see WhatsApp."
Analysis: Valid and consistent with the system's purpose (improving Nadia's operational efficiency). The request does not conflict with any existing requirement. Technical approach: Twilio WhatsApp Business API. The system already sends emails via SendGrid — adding WhatsApp notification is an additive change, not a structural one. VALID — proceed to Stage 2.

Stage 2 — Change Analysis & Costing
Impact on SRS: adds FR-024 "The system shall send a WhatsApp notification to the admin phone number when a new order is placed."
Impact on architecture: Twilio integration in the notification service (currently only SendGrid). Low coupling — notification service is already extracted.
Estimate: 2 days development (Kareem: Twilio API integration + test), 0.5 days QA.
Cost: Twilio WhatsApp per message: ~0.005 USD. At 50 orders/week: ~$0.25/week. Negligible.
Traceability: FR-024 traces to US-004 (order management) and the architectural notification service component.
Decision: APPROVED — add to Sprint 6 backlog as BP-037.

Stage 3 — Change Implementation
SRS v1.2 updated to include FR-024. ADR-003 created: "Use Twilio for WhatsApp notifications — same pattern as SendGrid for email." BP-037 added to product backlog. Implemented in Sprint 6. Nadia tested and confirmed working. SRS v1.2 signed 5 August 2024.
